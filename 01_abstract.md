# Abstract

Bycatch risk in dynamic shelf ecosystems depends on the changing overlap between marine predators, fishing activity, and ocean conditions. This project developed a reproducible daily bycatch riskscape framework for the Falkland Islands region, integrating environmental features, telemetry-derived species use, apparent fishing exposure, environmental plausibility, and map-based risk products on a common hexagonal spatial grid. The workflow covered 2014-2023 environmental and fishing conditions and used 2022-2023 telemetry observations for black-browed albatross and South American fur seal.

The final study grid contained 37,209 cells and 135.9 million daily environmental cell records. Processed fishing exposure included 3.09 million apparent fishing hours across 17,218 active cells, while the cleaned telemetry dataset contained 59,182 valid records from 42 tracked individuals and 76 trips. An Extra Trees model was selected as the primary species-use learner. Structured environmental validation using self-organizing-map hierarchical seascape classes showed that a 30-class grouped design provided the strongest tested validation framing. A Bayesian/Gaussian mixture component was retained separately as an environmental plausibility layer, allowing predictions made under weaker environmental support to be flagged without treating low plausibility as confirmed absence.

The resulting products distinguish predicted species use from realized risk and latent risk. Realized risk identifies where predicted species use overlapped observed fishing activity, whereas latent risk maps potential interaction risk independently of observed fishing exposure. Fishing activity was concentrated along shelf and shelf-break corridors and varied strongly through the seasonal cycle. Seascape-conditioned exploratory products retained broad seasonal and spatial structure but compressed localized high-use areas, indicating that seascapes are useful validation and interpretation layers but should not replace telemetry-informed prediction surfaces for fine-scale risk mapping.

The framework should be interpreted as a relative planning and interpretation tool rather than a calibrated bycatch-probability model. It establishes a shared spatial, temporal, and computational structure for future extensions, including broader telemetry coverage, independent observer validation, gear-specific risk calibration, stronger seascape integration, and forecasting products driven by recent or predicted environmental and fishing conditions.
